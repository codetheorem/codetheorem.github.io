A source is something that survived. An event is something that happened. The distance between them is where historical interpretation begins.

A letter records what a person chose to say to a particular recipient. An official account records what an institution considered worth preserving. An inscription presents a claim to an audience. None is a transparent copy of the surrounding world.

Recognizing that distance does not make historical knowledge impossible. It changes the questions we ask of evidence.

## Ask what kind of claim is available

Suppose an account says that a policy was widely accepted. Several propositions can hide inside that sentence: the author believed it was accepted; the author wanted a reader to believe it; the institution used that language publicly; or people actually consented.

The surviving statement directly supports some of these propositions more strongly than others. Moving from a statement to a conclusion about social reality requires an argument.

The distinction resembles a familiar problem in systems work. A log line is evidence that an execution emitted a message. Whether it establishes that an operation completed depends on where it was recorded, what failures were possible, and what the program meant by completion. The analogy is limited, but the discipline is useful: identify what the record actually warrants.

## Absence is difficult evidence

A missing account can have many explanations. Something may not have happened, may not have been recorded, may not have been preserved, or may not have been found. Those possibilities are not interchangeable.

The weight of absence depends on an expectation. If a kind of event was consistently recorded in a surviving collection, its absence there may matter. If the collection is fragmentary or selective, the same absence supports much less.

This is why historical arguments need an account of their sources, not simply a large number of citations. More references do not automatically repair a shared limitation in what those references can show.

## Resist retrospective necessity

Once an outcome is known, the preceding events can look as if they were arranging themselves to produce it. Choices become steps in a familiar story, and alternatives disappear from view.

An interpretation should try to recover the uncertainty available to the people involved. What information did they possess? Which possibilities remained open? Which categories were available in their own terms?

This does not require accepting every actor's self-description. It requires resisting an easy substitution: our knowledge of the ending for their experience of the situation.

## An honest account can remain incomplete

It is tempting to make an argument feel finished by smoothing over a gap. A more useful account can preserve the gap: this source establishes one thing, another source complicates it, and a further conclusion remains uncertain.

That kind of writing may be less decisive, but it gives the reader a way to judge the interpretation. The uncertainty belongs to the subject rather than being a defect to hide.

I am interested in historical reading for this discipline. It makes the relationship between evidence and explanation hard to ignore. A surviving record deserves attention, but it also deserves questions about its production, audience, and limits.
